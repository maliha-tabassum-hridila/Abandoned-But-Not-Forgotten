ABOUT THE PROJECT

WHY NASA LEAVES HARDWARE BEHIND

Every mission in this archive ended differently, but almost none of them ended by choice in the way people imagine. Spacecraft aren't recalled or driven home. They are built, launched, and then left to work until something — distance, darkness, dust, or simple time — brings the mission to a close. Here are the main reasons a NASA mission ends where it stands.

COMPLETED MISSIONS

The work was finished

Some hardware, like the Surveyor landers, was built to answer a specific question. Once that question was answered, the mission's job was done, whether or not the spacecraft kept transmitting afterward.

LOST CONTACT

The signal never came back

Missions like Opportunity and Pioneer 10 didn't fail so much as they simply stopped being reachable — buried in dust, out of power, or too far away for a signal to return.

ENVIRONMENTAL LIMITATIONS

The environment won

Extreme cold, punishing dust storms, and the dark of a polar winter have ended more Mars missions than any hardware failure. Spirit and Phoenix were both ultimately stopped by conditions their designers anticipated but couldn't fully protect against.

POWER LIMITATIONS

The batteries ran out

Solar panels lose power as dust settles on them; radioisotope generators lose power as their fuel naturally decays. Both are predictable, gradual processes — InSight and the Pioneer probes were both eventually silenced by fading power.

MISSION OBJECTIVES COMPLETED

They outlived their warranty

Many of these machines, including Spirit, Opportunity, and Sojourner, kept working for years past their original planned lifetimes, gathering far more science than anyone expected when they launched.

CONTINUING JOURNEYS

Some are still going

Voyager 1 and Voyager 2 aren't abandoned in the usual sense at all — they're still transmitting today, nearly five decades after launch, on a journey with no planned end.

"Abandoned" doesn't mean "meaningless." Every piece of hardware left behind is part of humanity's record of exploration.

ABOUT THIS SITE

Built for NASA Space Apps Challenge 2026

This site was built for the NASA Space Apps Challenge 2026 category "Abandoned but not Forgotten: Storytelling about NASA's Discarded Equipment on the Moon and Mars." It is an independent, non-official project intended for students, teachers, and space enthusiasts. Mission facts, dates, and figures are drawn from NASA and JPL mission pages; any first-person "if this machine could talk" text on mission pages is clearly labeled creative interpretation, not an actual transmission or NASA statement.

Built with HTML, CSS, and vanilla JavaScript — no framework, no backend and no required build step. The 3D scenes use Three.js (MIT licence), bundled in js/vendor/ so they work offline; the Hardware Map can show NASA basemap tiles over the internet and falls back to a built-in grid without it. Open index.html to explore (the 3D game needs a local web server; see the README).
